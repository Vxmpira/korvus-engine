SMT Divergence for Index Futures

Two correlated indexes usually move together. When one makes a new high and the other does not, that gap is called SMT divergence. This page explains what it is, how to find it, and how to review it honestly.

What SMT divergence means

SMT stands for Smart Money Technique. The idea is simple. Index futures that move together often confirm each other. When one index pushes to a new high or low and its partner fails to do the same, the two charts disagree.

That disagreement is the signal. It does not tell you what happens next. It only tells you the move is not fully shared.

ES and NQ as the classic pair

The most common pair is the S&P 500 future (ES) and the Nasdaq 100 future (NQ). They track broad US equity risk. They are not identical. NQ is more tech heavy and often moves more in points and percent.

Because they are closely linked, a clean split between them gets attention. If NQ prints a higher high and ES stalls under its prior high, you have a divergence to study.

Other pairs exist, including YM against ES, and cross market comparisons. The logic is the same. Pick two instruments with a known relationship, then watch where they stop agreeing.

How to spot it on a chart

  1. Open both charts on the same timeframe and time window. Align the candles.
  2. Mark the most recent swing high or low on each instrument.
  3. Watch for one instrument to take out its prior swing and the other to stop short.
  4. Note the time. A divergence that forms during a session window tends to be easier to review later.
  5. Write down what you saw before you look at what happened next.

Keep the rule plain. One side breaks, the other does not confirm. Anything more complicated tends to be the eye filling gaps.

Why it can signal weakness in a move

A rally that only one index is pushing is narrower than a rally both are pushing. Breadth is thinner. Fewer participants are behind the move.

That can mean the move is running on less support than it looks. It does not mean price must reverse. Divergences can persist. Some resolve by the other index catching up.

The signal is about agreement, not direction. A split tells you the picture is incomplete. Treat it as a question to answer, not an answer to act on.

Reviewing examples after the fact

Most of the learning happens in review. Build a simple log.

  • Date, session, and timeframe of the divergence.
  • Which instrument broke and which one stalled.
  • What the next 20 to 60 minutes did. Write it down without spin.
  • Whether the divergence was clean or messy. Be strict.
  • Count the misses as hard as the hits.

After a month of logging, you will know how often your own reads hold up. That number matters more than any single example.

Where session timing fits

A divergence in a quiet lunch chop means something different than one at a major open. Time context changes how much weight a split deserves. Read the session before you read the split.
