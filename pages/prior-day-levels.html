Prior Day High, Low and Settlement: The Levels Traders Mark Before the Open

Three numbers from yesterday. Most index futures traders mark them before the open anyway. Here is what they are, why price sometimes reacts there, and how to use them without guessing where price goes next.

What the three levels are

Prior day high is the highest traded price in the previous session's range. It is a fact, not an opinion. Once the session closes, it does not change.

Prior day low is the lowest traded price in that same range. Same rule. It is fixed.

Settlement is the official daily reference price the exchange publishes at the end of the session. It is set by the exchange's closing process, not by whoever happens to trade last. Many desks treat it as the single most watched price of the day, because margin, marks, and end of day reports all reference it.

Together, these three numbers give you a fixed map of yesterday. The market does not have to respect them. But everyone else can see them too.

Why traders watch them

A level matters when enough people act on it at the same time. Prior day levels are easy to see, easy to agree on, and sit in the same place for the whole next session. That is the reason they show up on so many charts.

Price has a history at those prices. Traders who were long or short near the prior high or low may have orders, stops, or mental reference points there. When price returns to a level that is already known, two things can happen. It can pause as orders are absorbed. Or it can move through as those orders are triggered. Both reactions are common. Neither is guaranteed.

How to mark them before the open

  1. Confirm the session definition your data uses. Overnight and regular session highs can differ. Pick one and stay with it.
  2. Mark the prior day high, prior day low, and settlement on your chart. Use plain lines. Label them.
  3. Write down the range size. Note whether the open sits above, below, or between the levels.
  4. Stop there. The levels are reference points, not a forecast.

How to use them without predicting

The honest use of these levels is to describe, not to call. Good questions sound like this:

  • Where is price relative to each level right now?
  • Did price hold, break, or stall at the last test?
  • Is volume and range changing as price approaches?
  • Did my own plan define what I would do at each level before I saw the reaction?

Bad questions sound like this: "It has to bounce here." "The low will break." Levels do not carry a promise. A level that held yesterday can fail today. A level that failed can be retested and hold. Nobody can know which in advance, and any page that says otherwise is selling something.

Common mistake. Treating every touch as a signal. A touch is only a touch. Without a defined plan, a level becomes an excuse to trade every time price wanders near it.
