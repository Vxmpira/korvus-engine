ICT Kill Zones: A Beginner Guide to Session Windows for Index Futures

Kill zones are time windows that ICT-style traders watch closely. This page explains what they are, which windows are commonly cited for index futures, and how to check the times on your own clock before you trust any of it.

Beginner level Index futures Time zones Education only

What a kill zone is

In the ICT framework, a kill zone is a window of time when price is often thought to move with more intent. The idea is simple: certain sessions open, overlap, or close, and the order flow around those moments can look different from the quiet hours between them.

That is the claim as practitioners describe it. It is a way of organizing attention, not a guarantee. Markets do not owe anyone a move at 8:30, and a window that looked active last week can be dead this week. Treat kill zones as a lens for watching the tape, and test the lens before you rely on it.

The commonly cited windows

Most ICT material uses New York time (Eastern) as the reference clock. The windows below are the ones you will see most often. Confirm them yourself before using them, because sources differ on exact start and end minutes.

How to verify the times yourself

Do not copy anyone's clock, including ours. Check it in four steps:

  1. Find the current New York time with a reliable time zone converter or your system clock set to America/New_York.
  2. Convert each window into your own local time. Write both down.
  3. Account for daylight saving changes. US and European clocks shift on different dates, so windows can move by an hour for a few weeks each year.
  4. Watch a live chart through one full window and note what happened. Your own observations matter more than any written summary.

If your converted times do not match a source, trust the arithmetic you did, then find out which source is wrong.

Where index futures fit

Index futures such as the E-mini S&P 500 and E-mini Nasdaq-100 trade nearly around the clock, with the heaviest activity during the US cash session. That is why the New York open window gets so much attention. Liquidity, news timing, and the session you trade from all shape what a window looks like on your screen.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Treating a window as a schedule of guaranteed moves.
  • Forgetting daylight saving shifts and trading an hour off.
  • Marking up every session on the chart until nothing is readable.
  • Judging a concept from three days of screenshots.
