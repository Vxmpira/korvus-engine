The economic calendar for futures traders, explained

Index futures move on data. Every month a handful of government and central bank releases land on a fixed schedule. This guide explains what four of the big ones measure, when they usually hit, and how to read the numbers without the hype.

What an economic calendar is

An economic calendar is a list of scheduled releases. Each row names an event, the country or agency behind it, a time, and usually three numbers: the actual result, the forecast, and the previous reading.

Forecasts come from surveys of economists. They are a consensus guess, not a promise. The market usually prices in the forecast before the release. The price move tends to come from the gap between the actual number and that guess.

That gap is the whole game for news traders. A number that matches the forecast can still move price, because the surrounding story changed. A number far from the forecast can fade within minutes. The calendar tells you when the question gets answered. It does not tell you the answer.

How to read one row

  • Time. Most US releases print at 8:30 AM Eastern. Check your own timezone before the morning, not during it.
  • Impact. Calendars rank events as low, medium or high. High impact usually means the market has a reason to care right now.
  • Actual, forecast, previous. Read the actual against the forecast first. Then compare it to the previous month to see the direction of travel.
  • Revisions. Previous numbers are sometimes revised. A revision can change the story even when the headline looks flat.

CPI: Consumer Price Index

CPI tracks the prices households pay for a basket of goods and services, including food, energy, rent and transport. The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes it monthly.

Traders watch CPI because inflation shapes what the Federal Reserve does with interest rates. Hotter inflation tends to raise the odds of tighter policy. Cooler inflation tends to raise the odds of easier policy. Markets react to those odds, and index futures often react fast.

Core CPI strips out food and energy, which swing a lot. Many traders read core first. Headline CPI still gets the attention in the first seconds of a release.

PPI: Producer Price Index

PPI measures prices at the producer level, the costs businesses see before they reach shoppers. It is also published monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

PPI is often treated as an early signal. If producers face higher costs, some of that may reach consumer prices later. Some traders weight PPI heavily. Others see it as a secondary read that rarely beats CPI on the day. Both views are common. Neither is a rule.

NFP: Nonfarm Payrolls

Nonfarm Payrolls, usually shortened to NFP, counts the change in US jobs outside farming. It comes out on the first Friday of most months, and it is one of the most watched releases of the month.

Strong job growth can signal an economy that does not need rate cuts. Weak growth can signal the opposite. The unemployment rate and wage growth print in the same report, and traders often look at wages for inflation clues.

NFP can be noisy. Revisions to earlier months are common, and the first print is often revised later. Treat the first number as a snapshot, not a verdict.

FOMC: the Federal Open Market Committee

The FOMC is the Federal Reserve's policy committee. It meets about eight times a year. At the end of each meeting it issues a statement on interest rates, and the Fed chair usually holds a press conference shortly after.

The rate decision is only part of the event. The wording of the statement, the updated economic projections, and the tone of the press conference can all move markets. Many traders say the words matter as much as the decision. Some moves happen during the press conference, not at the statement itself.

FOMC days often carry more volatility than a standard data print. Volatility cuts both ways. Wider swings can produce larger losses as easily as larger gains.

Why timing matters more than you think

Data drops at fixed times, and liquidity often thins right before them. Spreads can widen. Fills can slip. Price can jump several points in a single second. A beginner who has never watched a release live often underestimates how fast the book changes.

Many experienced traders simply know the schedule and plan around it. Some step aside for the high-impact prints. Others stay in the market and accept the risk. Both choices are personal. The calendar does not decide it for you.

A simple prep habit

Before each week, write down the high-impact events and their times in your local zone. Note which ones touch your market. Decide ahead of time whether you will watch, wait or ignore. That habit costs nothing and prevents most of the morning scramble.

Keep notes after each release too. Record the actual, the forecast, and how price behaved in the first few minutes. Over a few months you build a reference that is specific to your own market and your own habits.
