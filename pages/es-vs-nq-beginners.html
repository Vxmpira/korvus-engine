ES vs NQ: what the two most-watched index futures actually are

If you are new to index futures, ES and NQ are the two names you will hear first. They look similar on a screen. They are not the same product. This guide explains what each one tracks, how tick size and tick value differ, and why NQ usually moves more points in a session. It is education, not a recommendation to trade either one.

Education only Specs as listed by CME Figures marked Illustration

What each contract tracks

Both contracts are E-mini index futures traded on the CME. An index futures contract is an agreement tied to the value of a stock index, not to a single company. You never own the stocks. You trade the price of the index.

Ticker: ES

E-mini S&P 500

Tracks the S&P 500, a broad basket of roughly 500 large US companies across many sectors. It is the usual benchmark for the overall US stock market.

Ticker: NQ

E-mini Nasdaq-100

Tracks the Nasdaq-100, a basket of 100 large non-financial companies listed on the Nasdaq. It is heavily weighted toward technology and growth names.

Tick value, worked through

A tick is the smallest price step a contract can move. For both ES and NQ that step is 0.25 index points. The dollar value of a tick depends on the point value of the contract. Multiply the tick size by the point value and you get the tick value.

ES: 0.25 points times $50 equals $12.50 per tick. NQ: 0.25 points times $20 equals $5.00 per tick. The same 0.25 point step is worth more dollars in ES, because each ES point carries a larger multiplier.

Dollar figures in this guide are per contract, before fees, commissions, and slippage. They show arithmetic, not expected results.

Volatility: why NQ looks busier

Volatility means how far price travels over a period. Two things drive the difference between these contracts. NQ is built from fewer, faster-moving companies, and the Nasdaq-100 level is a much larger number than the S&P 500 level, so the same percentage move produces more index points. A session that moves a few percent looks very different in points depending on which index you are watching.

The practical point for a beginner is simple. A one point move is not the same thing in both contracts. Before you size anything, convert points to dollars using the table above, and remember that a bigger point range in NQ does not automatically mean a bigger dollar result, since each NQ point is worth less.

What the numbers do not tell you

Tick value and point value are arithmetic. They do not show how often price moves, when it moves, or in which direction. They also do not include margin requirements, which change over time, or the costs of trading. Read the contract specs, read the margin rules from your broker, and treat any single chart as one piece of information out of many.

Nothing on this page predicts where either index will go. Both contracts can lose money quickly. If you are new, learn the mechanics on paper or in a simulator first.
