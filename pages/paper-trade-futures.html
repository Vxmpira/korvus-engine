How to paper trade index futures

Paper trading means placing simulated trades with no real money at risk. Done well, it teaches you your process before the market charges you for lessons. Done badly, it teaches you habits that fall apart the first time real money is on the line. This guide covers the difference.

Beginner level Reading time about 8 min Education only, not advice

What paper trading is

Paper trading is practice trading in a simulated account. You see live or delayed prices, pick a contract such as ES or NQ, and enter and exit trades as if they were real. The fills, profit and loss, and account balance are tracked by the software, but no money moves.

The point is not to make fake money. The point is to test a written plan, learn how your platform behaves, and find out where your own decisions go wrong while the cost is zero.

Paper trading does not tell you whether a strategy will work with real money. It tells you whether you can follow your rules, read the chart you chose, and use the tools without fumbling. Those are real skills, but they are only part of the job.

Simulated versus live: what changes

The gap between a simulated account and a live one is where most beginners get surprised. The table below lists the usual differences. Treat it as a checklist to read, not a forecast of your results.

Money at riskNone. Losses are only numbers.Real capital. Every loss is paid from your account.
FillsOften filled at the price you see. Can look cleaner than reality.Filled where the order actually executes, which can differ from the price you expected.

How long to sim

There is no magic number, and anyone who gives you one is selling something. What matters is whether your sample is big enough and varied enough to say anything at all.

A few sensible checks before you move on:

  • You have written your plan down and followed it on most trades, not just the ones that felt good.
  • You have logged enough trades to see patterns in your own behavior, not just a lucky week.
  • Your sample includes different conditions: quiet sessions, fast sessions, news days, and days when nothing worked.
  • You can explain why each trade was taken and why each one was exited, in plain words.
  • Your simulated results still look reasonable after you subtract realistic commissions and assume some slippage.

If you have only traded a handful of sessions, you have not simmed long enough. If you have traded for months and cannot describe your own rules, the time has not been used well.

Common mistakes beginners repeat

These show up over and over. None of them is unique to index futures, and all of them are cheap to fix while you are still on paper.

01

Treating sim fills as real fills

Paper fills are often cleaner than live ones. Build in a slippage assumption and a commission cost before you trust any result.

02

Sizing up because it is free

Trading five contracts on paper teaches nothing about trading one contract live. Practice at the size you plan to trade.

03

Changing the rules mid trade

Moving a stop because the trade feels bad is the habit you most need to catch early. Write the rule first, then follow it.

04

Judging by one good week

A short streak says more about variance than skill. Look at the full log, including the weeks that hurt.

05

Skipping the journal

Without notes on why you entered and exited, you cannot separate a process problem from bad luck.

06

Ignoring the calendar

Scheduled releases and thin sessions change how price moves. Note the context of each trade, not just the chart.

Moving to live, carefully

Going live is a change in conditions, not a graduation. Assume your first live weeks will feel different from your sim, and plan for that instead of hoping it will not.

  1. Start with the smallest contract size your broker offers for the instrument you practiced.
  2. Keep the same written plan. Do not add new setups or new markets in the first stretch.
  3. Set a loss limit you can absorb and decide in advance what you will do when you hit it, such as stopping for the day.
  4. Log live trades the same way you logged sim trades, and compare the two honestly.
  5. Increase size only after a long, documented period in which your behavior matches your plan, not just your P&L.

Before you go live

Confirm your platform, your data feed, your fee schedule, and your margin requirements. Know how to close a position quickly.

After you go live

Review each session. Note where the fill differed from the sim, what you felt, and whether you followed the plan.
