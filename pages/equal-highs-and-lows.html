Equal Highs and Lows Explained for Index Futures

Equal highs and equal lows are swing points on a chart that sit at, or very close to, the same price. Traders who study market structure mark them because they are easy to see and because they repeat. This page explains what the term means, why some traders study these levels, and how to review them on your own charts.

Market structure Index futures Education only

What the term means

A swing high is a local peak: a candle with lower highs on both sides. A swing low is a local trough with higher lows on both sides. When two swing highs print at nearly the same price, that pair is called equal highs. When two swing lows line up the same way, that pair is called equal lows.

The word "equal" is loose in practice. Two highs a few ticks apart on an ES or NQ chart are usually grouped together. Your own rule for what counts as close enough should be written down before you start marking them, so you are not adjusting it after the fact.

Why traders study them

Many traders describe equal highs and equal lows as places where resting orders may be clustered. Stop orders are often placed just beyond an obvious high or low, so a sweep through that level is sometimes discussed as liquidity being taken. This is a concept taught in several market structure frameworks, not a guaranteed pattern. Price does not have to react to these levels, and it often ignores them.

For a deeper look at how resting orders are discussed around price levels, see resting liquidity.

How to review them on a chart

  • Pick a timeframe and mark only swing points you can defend with a written rule.
  • Write down your tolerance for "equal" in ticks or points, and keep it fixed.
  • Note whether the level was swept, closed beyond, or held, and label it that way.
  • Check whether a higher timeframe level sits at the same price. Overlap is worth noting.
  • Review past examples in replay. Count how often you would have acted and how often you would not.

Where they fit with other concepts

Equal highs and lows are often studied alongside divergence between correlated index futures. When one contract makes a new extreme and a related contract does not confirm it, that is a separate observation. The SMT divergence page covers that idea on its own terms.
