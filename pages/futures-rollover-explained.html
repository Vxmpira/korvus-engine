Futures rollover explained

Index futures do not last forever. Every contract has an expiration date, and the market moves its attention to the next one before that date arrives. This guide explains the cycle, the vocabulary, and why charts look different on either side of a roll.

The quarterly cycle

The popular index futures contracts, including E-mini S&P 500 (ES), E-mini Nasdaq-100 (NQ), Micro E-mini S&P 500 (MES), and Micro E-mini Nasdaq-100 (MNQ), trade on a quarterly schedule. Four contracts are listed at any time, and each one ends in a month that carries a fixed letter code.

The micro contracts follow the same month codes. MES and MNQ are written as MES and MNQ followed by the month letter and year digit, so a June 2030 micro S&P contract is written MESM30. The letters are a convention for naming. They do not describe the contract itself.

What expiration means

Each quarterly contract has a last trading day, which falls on the third Friday of its contract month. After that day the contract stops trading and settles. Index futures of this type are cash settled, so no shares change hands. Your position is closed out against the final settlement price and the account is adjusted in cash.

Before expiration, a trader who wants to keep exposure to the index has to move to a later contract. That move is what people call a roll.

What front month means

The front month is the nearest contract that has not yet expired. It is the one most people mean when they say "the ES" or "the NQ" without naming a month.

In practice, the front month is usually the contract with the most volume and open interest. That is not a rule written into the exchange, and it can change. The front month is a description of where activity sits at a given moment, and that position moves forward on a schedule.

Roll week and the volume shift

In the days before expiration, volume tends to migrate from the expiring contract to the next one. Traders who hold positions close them in the old contract and open matching positions in the new one. Market makers and institutions do the same on their own schedules. Over a few sessions, the bulk of activity moves across.

The timing varies. Some participants roll early, others wait until the final days. The shift is gradual in most cases, though the exact pattern changes from quarter to quarter.

Why charts gap across contracts

Two contracts on the same index can trade at different prices on the same day. The gap comes from the cost of carrying a position until each expiration, which reflects interest rates, expected dividends on the underlying stocks, and time. Contracts that expire later usually sit at a different level than the nearer one. This difference is often called the basis or spread.

When a chart switches from the expiring contract to the next one, the price history can show a jump at the switch point. That jump is a difference between two instruments, not a move in the market itself. It can look like a gap, a spike, or an odd level that never traded.

Continuous and actual contracts

There are two ways to look at a futures series.

Actual contract

A single contract, such as the June contract, with only its own trades and prices. It starts trading, has an active life, and then expires. Its history stops at expiration.

Continuous contract

A stitched series that joins many actual contracts into one line over time. It is convenient for long charts, but the joins create the gaps described above. Some continuous series adjust old prices to remove the jumps, and some do not. Knowing which one you are looking at matters when you read older levels.

When a level appears on a continuous chart, check which contract produced it. A level from an expired contract may not exist on the current one.

Practical reading notes

  • Confirm the contract month shown in your platform before reading a price level.
  • Expect a difference between contracts during and around roll week.
  • Treat a jump on a continuous chart as a possible contract switch before treating it as a market event.
  • Micro and standard contracts share the same expiration schedule, so both roll in the same window.
